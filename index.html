How to Choose a China Sourcing Agent: 9 Checks Before You Pay

Quick Answer

To choose a China sourcing agent, check nine things before you pay: where they are based, their business registration, references, how they charge, whether suppliers pay them commission, how they report, what they do in person, their refund terms, and how quickly they reply. An agent paid only by you has no reason to push one supplier over another.

Why the Choice Matters More Than the Price

Hiring an agent is one of the fastest ways to get a Chinese order moving. It is also one of the easiest ways to lose money if you pick the wrong person. Many buyers only learn what kind of agent they hired after a deposit has gone out and the quotes start to look oddly similar.

This guide gives you nine checks to run before you pay anyone. They take an afternoon, and they tell you whether the person on the other end works for you or for the factory.

What a Sourcing Agent in China Actually Does

A sourcing agent in China is a person or small company that acts as your local representative. They find suppliers that fit your product, check that those suppliers are real, collect quotes, arrange samples, visit factories, follow up on production and help with shipping.

You do not need one for every order. If you are buying a few units of a standard item from a supplier you already trust, you can probably manage alone. The picture changes when the order is large, the product is custom, the supplier is new to you, or you cannot read Mandarin and cannot be in China. That is when it makes sense to hire a sourcing agent, because one mistake costs more than the agent's fee.

Check 1: Where Is the Agent Based?

An agent sitting in your own country and passing your request to someone in China is a middleman, not a local representative. Look for someone who lives and works near the factories, ideally in a manufacturing and trade hub such as Guangzhou, and who can reach a supplier's workshop in hours rather than days.

Ask where they are physically based, and ask for a video call from their office. Someone who is genuinely on the ground will not mind. The call also shows you how well they speak English and how clearly they explain things, which matters when you will be relying on them for months.

Check 2: Can They Show a Real, Registered Business?

A legitimate agent runs a registered company, or can at least explain clearly how they operate. Ask for the company name and registration details, and check them yourself. A real business has a name that matches its website, its invoices and its bank account.

Be careful with anyone who works only through a messaging app, has no website, and asks you to send money to a personal account. A registered company can issue proper invoices and contracts. A personal account gives you very little to fall back on if something goes wrong.

Check 3: Do They Have References and Past Work?

Ask for two or three buyers you can speak to, ideally in your own country and in a similar product category. A good agent will have them ready. They may also share anonymised examples of past work, such as a quote comparison, a supplier check report or an inspection summary.

Pay attention to how specific the examples are. Vague praise such as great service tells you nothing. A report that lists what was checked, what was found and what was recommended tells you how the agent actually works.

Check 4: How Do They Get Paid?

There are a few common ways agents charge. Some take a percentage of your order value. Some charge a flat fee for a project. Others charge by the hour or by task, such as one supplier check or one factory visit.

None of these is automatically wrong, but you should know which one you are buying, and the total should be clear before you start. If you cannot get a straight answer on how the agent earns money, treat that as your answer. Ask for a full breakdown in writing, including anything that could be added later.

Check 5: Do Suppliers Pay Them Commission?

This is the check that matters most, and the one most buyers skip.

Plenty of agents earn a quiet commission from the factory, on top of or instead of a fee from you. The supplier builds that commission into your price. The agent then has a reason to steer you toward the supplier that pays best, not the one that suits you best.

An independent sourcing agent is paid by the buyer only and takes nothing from suppliers. That removes the conflict. Ask directly: do you receive any payment, discount or rebate from suppliers on my order? Ask for the answer in writing. Canton Fair Sourcing, for example, states that the supplier pays it no commission on your order, so its only job is to work for you.

Check 6: How Will They Report Back?

You will not be standing in the factory, so you rely on the agent's reports. Ask what a typical update looks like and how often you will get one.

Good reporting includes photos and video taken on the day, written findings with clear pass or fail points, and a short summary of what you need to decide next. Poor reporting is a one-line message saying everything is fine. Ask to see a sample report before you start, and check that it names the specific things that were looked at, not just the conclusion.

Check 7: What Will They Do in Person?

List the tasks you need, then ask which of them the agent will do themselves and which they will hand to someone else. Typical in-person work includes checking that a supplier is a real factory, collecting and comparing quotes, receiving and testing samples, inspecting goods before they ship, and managing the order from start to finish.

Some buyers need only one of these. Others want the whole order run for them. Pick an agent who can do the level you need, and who is honest about what falls outside their experience. An agent who says yes to everything is as risky as one who says very little.

Check 8: What Are the Refund Terms?

Read the terms before you send money. Look for what is covered, what happens if you are unhappy with the work, and whether you can get your money back.

A clear refund promise is a good sign of confidence. Canton Fair Sourcing, for instance, offers a 100% refund if you are not satisfied. Whatever the agent promises, make sure it is written into the agreement and not only into a chat message. If the terms are vague, ask for them to be tightened before you begin.

Check 9: How Fast Do They Reply, and Will They Do a Small Test Job?

Send a real question and see how long the answer takes. A reply within one working day is a reasonable standard, and it is the response time Canton Fair Sourcing quotes. Slow, vague replies at the sales stage rarely get better once you have paid.

Then start small. Give them one task, such as checking a single supplier or comparing three quotes. A paid test job costs little, and you will learn more from it than from any sales call.

How to Find a Sourcing Agent in China

If you are wondering how to find a sourcing agent in China, start with a few reliable routes:

  • Search for agents based in a trade hub, and read their own websites, not only listings on third-party sites.
  • Ask other importers in your product category who they use.
  • Look at agents who attend the Canton Fair, since they work with exhibitors every season.
  • Shortlist two or three and run the nine checks on each.

Compare them on the same checklist rather than on price alone. A cheaper agent who takes hidden commission usually costs more in the end.

So Who Is the Best Sourcing Agent in China?

There is no single best sourcing agent in China for every buyer. The best one for you is the one whose business model, location and skills match your product and order size, and who passes the nine checks above. Treat any site that crowns one agent as number one with caution, because those lists are often paid for.

Red Flags to Walk Away From

  • No company name, website or physical address.
  • Payment requested to a personal bank account.
  • A refusal to say how they earn money.
  • Pressure to pay a large deposit straight away.
  • Promises of the lowest price on every product.
  • No references, no sample report and no written terms.

Final Thoughts

Choosing well comes down to a simple idea: know who pays the agent, and know what you are getting for that money. Run the nine checks, start with a small job, and keep the terms in writing.
